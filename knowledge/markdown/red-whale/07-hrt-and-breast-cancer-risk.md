# HRT and breast cancer risk

> **Layer 1 Source Reference:** [`HRT and breast cancer risk 7.docx`](../../sources/red-whale/HRT%20and%20breast%20cancer%20risk%207.docx)
> **Collection:** Red Whale Menopause Guidelines | **Original Format:** `DOCX` | **Review Date:** `June 2025`

In its guideline on menopause, NICE advises us that taking HRT increases a woman’s risk of developing breast cancer – combined preparations more so than oestrogen only (NICE 2015 (updated 2024) [NG23](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23)).

However, the relationship between breast cancer and HRT is more complicated than this:

- Breast cancer is not a single disease entity.
- HRT is not a single pharmacological class.
- Individual risk is impacted by other (potentially more important) factors.

Since the Women’s Health Initiative and the Million Women studies, further research has examined the risk of breast cancer with specific types of HRT. This article aims to clarify the situation so that we can counsel patients appropriately.

*This article was updated in June 2025.*

## Landmark studies on HRT and breast cancer: WHI and WMS

The Million Women Study and Women’s Health Initiative were landmark studies at the time of publication. Their findings have since been questioned.

| Study detail | Findings |

Blown out of the water…

Subsequent epidemiological analyses of the data above suggest the evidence was unreliable, highlighting defects in study design, confounding factors, bias, lack of consistency and time order ([J Fam Plann Reprod Health Care 2011;37(4):225](https://doi.org/10.1136/jfprhc-2011-0091), [2011;37(3):165](https://doi.org/10.1136/jfprhc-2011-0090), [2012;38(2):102](https://doi.org/10.1136/jfprhc-2011-100229)).

## Subsequent epidemiological meta-analysis data

A large meta-analysis which received a lot of media attention examined breast cancer risk associated with various types and durations of HRT use. The data included 108 647 breast cancer cases, which were matched to up to 4 controls ([Lancet 2019;394:1159](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)31709-X/fulltext)).

### Findings

The main findings were that breast cancer risk:

- Is increased even in women using HRT <5y (although longer use is associated with greater risk).
- Is increased even in women starting HRT under the age of 50y (although the numbers in this subgroup were small, and these women were compared with age-matched menopausal women who would be at lower risk of breast cancer, NOT normal cycling women).
- Remains higher for up to 10–15y AFTER stopping HRT (although other data shows risk reduction once HRT is stopped).
- Is different between different types of HRT (although there was no data for micronised progesterone).

### The numbers

So, *assuming the association is causal*, this data suggests that for women of average weight starting HRT at age 50y, breast cancer risk over 5 years would be increased from 6.3% to:

- 8.3% for continuous combined HRT, which is an absolute increase of 1 in 50 users.
- 7.7% for cyclical combined HRT, which is an absolute increase of 1 in 70 users.
- 6.8% for oestrogen-only HRT, which is an absolute increase of 1 in 200 users.

No increased risk from vaginal oestrogens alone was seen.

### British Menopause Society evaluation

The [BMS, while welcoming more data to help guide women and their clinicians, responded](https://www.menohealth.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/BMS-HRT-statement.pdf) that:

- The study looks only at breast cancer incidence, not mortality (which may be unaffected).
- The data should be considered in the context of symptom relief, improved quality of life and the reduction in cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis associated with HRT use.

## How might HRT cause breast cancer?

Lifetime oestrogen exposure impacts a woman’s risk of developing breast cancer ([Climacteric, 2019;22 (1):3):](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2017.1388364)

- Oestrogens have a proliferative effect on breast tissue, and increased cell division gives rise to greater chance of cell mutations and therefore cancer development.
- Some oestrogen metabolites are directly genotoxic, leading to DNA damage and cell mutations.

And yet, evidence increasingly shows that breast cancer risk is higher in women on combined HRT than oestrogen-only regimens. *Confused?*

A review paper may help to explain this ([Ann Oncology 2025;36:134](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0923753424048804)):

- Oestrogens have a proliferative effect on breast tissue, but so does progesterone.
- Oestrogen appears to induce progesterone receptors in breast tissue, amplifying their effect.
- It is the combination of oestrogen and progestogen in HRT which carries greatest increased risk, although the risk may depend on the type of progestogen (see below).

### HRT: initiator or promoter?

Data suggests that HRT increases breast cancer risk within months or a few years of treatment, and the risk appears to reduce on discontinuation of treatment. If HRT does induce breast cancer development de-novo (via one of the mechanisms above), one would expect the rise in breast cancer incidence to occur much later after starting the drug, and that incidence wouldn’t decline immediately on stopping.

A paper from Maturitas suggests that HRT may stimulate growth of an already present breast tumour which would have otherwise gone undiagnosed or presented later, i.e. HRT is a cancer cell promotor not an initiator ([Maturitas, 2010;65:183](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.maturitas.2009.11.005)). The author postulates that HRT may not cause breast cancer but just lead to earlier detection of existing disease.

*Clearly, more research is needed!*

## What is the evidence that oestrogen-only HRT reduces the risk of breast cancer?

This comes from ONE study: the oestrogen-only arm of the WHI.

- Initial results found a non-significant reduced risk of breast cancer among 5310 women receiving 0.625mg conjugated equine oestrogens compared with placebo ([JAMA 2004;291:1701](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.291.14.1701)).
- Long-term (around 20y) follow-up of these women found a statistically significant lower risk of breast cancer incidence and mortality ([JAMA 2020;324 (4):369](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.9482)):
  - Incidence: HR = 0.78 (CI 0.65–0.93).
  - Mortality: HR = 0.60 (CI 0.37–0.97).

### How is that biologically plausible?

The reduced risk of breast cancer in the WHI oestrogen-only arm may be because:

- The study included an older population, and oestrogen may not affect breast cancer risk in older postmenopausal women in the same way as in younger ones.
- The changes in breast density in women on HRT may reduce the sensitivity of mammographic screening.

([Lancet 2019;394:1159](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)31709-X/fulltext), [Climacteric, 2019;22 (1):3](https://doi.org/10.1080/13697137.2017.1388364))

However, other data is conflicting.

## Which progestogens are safest for breast tissue?

The effect of progestogens on the breast appears complicated and is poorly understood.

Progestogens vary in their chemical structure and pharmacokinetics, so it is likely that the different progestogens affect breast tissue differently. In vitro data looking at the effect of different progestogens at cellular level is conflicting and has not been able to establish the predominant effect of a specific progestogen on breast cells.

### Is an IUS safer in terms of breast cancer risk?

No.

- While it would seem plausible that the localised delivery of levonorgestrel would be safer than systemic progestogen, there is no evidence (so far) that route of hormone administration impacts breast cancer risk.
- Data from the Finnish Cancer Registry has suggested similar risks of breast cancer with HRT regimens using an IUS and those using oral progestogens ([International Journal of Cancer,2009;126:483](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijc.24738)).
- Observational evidence has shown an association between IUS use (whatever the indication) and a slightly increased risk of breast cancer, which is higher in women >50y.
- However, this data was subjected to methodological flaws, and confounding factors may have greatly impacted the results ([Acta Obstet Gynecol Scand. 2020;99:970](https://doi.org/10.1111/aogs.13817)).

In summary, data suggests that HRT formulations containing micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone may be safer from a breast cancer point of view, but more evidence is needed to clarify exact risks.

## Counselling regarding breast cancer risk and HRT

Studies have (fairly) consistently reported that the addition of progestogens to oestrogen increases the risk of breast cancer, and oestrogen-only HRT almost certainly does not increase the risk of breast cancer to the same degree.

NICE recommends we advise women without a personal history of breast cancer that ([NICE 2015 (updated 2024) NG23](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23/chapter/recommendations#reviewing-treatment-for-anyone)):

- There is an association between HRT use and breast cancer, with some formulations appearing to carry more risk than others.
  - Risk is higher with continuous combined HRT than sequential HRT (*but remember that risk of endometrial cancer rises with prolonged sequential regimen use*).
  - Risk is lower with oestrogen-only HRT compared with combined HRT, but this is not appropriate for women with an intact uterus due to the risk of endometrial carcinoma.
- Breast cancer risk rises with duration of HRT use and declines after stopping, but persists at least 10 years after use.
- There is very little, or no, increased risk of developing or dying from breast cancer with oestrogen-only HRT.
- We should consider this risk in the context of an individual’s other risk factors.

To support with this discussion, NICE has produced a document titled [HRT and the likelihood of some medical conditions – a discussion aid for GP and patients](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23/resources/incidence-of-medical-conditions-with-and-without-hrt-a-discussion-aid-pdf-13553199901). This contains visual decision aids displaying the likelihood of developing a particular disease on HRT compared with non-use. Below, we have summarised the data on breast cancer:

## HRT compared with other risk factors for breast cancer

- Breast cancer is the commonest cancer in the UK.
- A woman born in the UK has a 1 in 7 lifetime risk of developing breast cancer.
- Age is the biggest risk factor for breast cancer: 25% of new cases are in women ≥75y, and peak incidence rates are in women in their 90s (*when ‘current HRT user’ is unlikely to be a factor!*).
- In terms of other attributable risks for breast cancer:
  - 2% of cases are caused by postmenopausal hormones.
  - 5% of cases are caused by not breastfeeding.
  - 8% of cases are caused by alcohol drinking.
  - 8% of cases are caused by overweight and obesity.

([Cancer Research UK - breast cancer risk](https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/health-professional/cancer-statistics/statistics-by-cancer-type/breast-cancer#heading-Four) accessed May 2025))

- Avoidance of modifiable risk factors could potentially prevent almost a quarter of breast cancers ([Post Reproductive Health, 2020;26(3)126](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/minc/26/3)).

The Lancet study found ([Lancet 2019;394:1159](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)31709-X/fulltext)):

- Little additional breast cancer risk for HRT among women who were obese (presumably because of high endogenous oestrogen production in adipose tissue).
- The estimated 20y incidence of breast cancer for a 50-year-old woman with a BMI >30 and NOT on HRT was higher than an average-weight woman who used oestrogen for 5y.

## HRT for women with a family history of breast cancer

In women with a familial risk or high-risk benign breast condition:

- Lifestyle and non-hormonal alternatives should be used as first-line management of vasomotor symptoms in high-risk women.
- If symptoms are severe or refractory, HRT may be considered on an individual basis following full discussion of risks and benefits ([Post Reproductive Health, 2019; 25(1):33](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053369119825716)).

The NICE guideline on managing familial breast cancer agrees that the decision to prescribe HRT to a higher-risk woman should be taken on an individual basis according to severity of symptoms ([CG164](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg164), 2013).

An epidemiological modelling study looked at the risk of developing, and dying from, breast cancer for women with different ‘severity’ of family history risk when taking different HRT types ([BJGP 2024;2023.0327](https://bjgp.org/content/early/2024/05/04/BJGP.2023.0327/tab-article-info?versioned=true)).

- HRT use had an additive effect on the risk of both diagnosis of and death from breast cancer in women with a family history of the disease.
- The absolute risk increase from HRT use is small when compared with the risk increase from family history.
- For a woman of ‘average’ family history, use of sequential combined HRT over 5 years increases the absolute risk of dying from breast cancer by 0.1%. For a woman with a strong family history (2 first-degree relatives affected by age 60y), this risk increase is 0.3% when compared with no HRT.
- The greater the exposure to progestogen, alongside oestrogen, the greater the risk. Therefore, the risk is lower with oestrogen-only preparations, and sequential regimens confer lower risk than continuous combined.

The model created 4 breast cancer risk levels. It is important to note that these thresholds are NOT the same as the low, moderate and high-risk thresholds for genetic screening due to family history outlined by NICE in its guidance on familial breast cancer (NICE 2013, [CG164](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg164), updated 2022). You can read more about these in our article *Breast cancer: genetic risk.*

- Average: a woman with no specific family history.
- Modest: a woman with a single first-degree relative diagnosed with breast cancer at age 60y.
- Intermediate: a woman with a single first-degree relative diagnosed at age 40y.
- Strong: a woman with 2 first-degree relatives diagnosed at age 50y.

The study looked at the risks for combined (all types) HRT, continuous progestogen and oestrogen-only regimens. It did not look at micronised progesterone.

**Cumulative risk of breast cancer by age 80y according to severity of family breast cancer risk**

| Category | Average family risk | Modest family risk | Intermediate family risk | Strong family risk |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Baseline risk of breast cancer death** | 1 in 58 | 1 in 43 | 1 in 37 | 1 in 31 |
| **Additional deaths after 5 years of combined (all types) HRT use age 50–55y** | 1 in 642 | 1 in 429 | 1 in 350 | 1 in 286 |
| **Additional deaths after 5 years of oestrogen-only HRT use age 50–55y** | 1 in 1406 | 1 in 938 | 1 in 761 | 1 in 621 |

The authors note that for a woman with a family history of breast cancer, most of her breast cancer risk will be attributable to family history, and there is only a small absolute increase in risk from use of HRT. This increased risk may or may not be acceptable to the individual woman. This model may be helpful for discussions about use of HRT in women with a family history of breast cancer.

## Key Clinical Summary

> **HRT and breast cancer risk**
> Evidence suggests that HRT use is associated with an increased risk of breast cancer – combined more so than oestrogen-only preparations.
> Due to the pattern of breast cancer incidence in relation to HRT use, it has been postulated that HRT acts more as a disease promotor rather than as a carcinogen.
> The WHI study is the only study which has shown a reduced risk of breast cancer with oestrogen-only HRT, and overall evidence suggests there may be a slightly increased risk.
> Micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear to be safer progestogens in terms of breast cancer risk.
> The risk of breast cancer associated with HRT use is lower than the risk associated with other life-style risk factors, e.g. obesity and alcohol.

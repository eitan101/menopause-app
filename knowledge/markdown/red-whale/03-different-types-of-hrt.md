# Different types of HRT: bioidentical, conjugated and synthetic

> **Layer 1 Source Reference:** [`3 Different types of HRT_ bioidentical, conjugated and synthetic.docx`](../../sources/red-whale/3%20Different%20types%20of%20HRT_%20bioidentical%2C%20conjugated%20and%20synthetic.docx)
> **Collection:** Red Whale Menopause Guidelines | **Original Format:** `DOCX` | **Review Date:** `November 2024`

## So, what should I prescribe?!

There are a myriad of different HRT formulations listed in the BNF and it can be very confusing choosing an appropriate treatment, particularly when a menopausal woman is sat in front of you expectantly! Moreover, in recent years, some popular formulations have disappeared off the market (e.g. Prempak C, Angeliq) and others have experienced availability issues (e.g. lots of patches!).

There is currently no recommended or established first-line option for HRT. However:

Regimens involving transdermal oestrogen with oral micronised progesterone are increasingly considered as preferred options due to better safety and efficacy compared with other types.

The aim of this article is to clarify the properties of all the different HRT preparations:

- Conventional (oestradiol and progestogen-based) formulations.
- HRT containing conjugated equine oestrogens.
- Compounded and regulated ‘bioidentical’ hormones.
- Tibolone.

While reading this article, you may wish to look at the different brands of currently available HRT formulations, a list of which can be found in the *HRT: GEMS* – so you know what’s what.

There is also a separate article on the practicalities of *HRT: a guide to starting, troubleshooting and stopping.*

*This article was updated in November 2024.*

## Types of HRT

Dropping oestrogen levels cause menopausal symptoms, and HRT is designed to top up these levels. There are four main types of HRT:

## Conventional (oestradiol and progestogen-based) formulations

Conventional HRT contains oestrogen and a progestogen, and is usually the first go-to HRT option.

### Oestrogen

- Is the therapeutic agent.
- This may be oestradiol or oestradiol valerate.
- These are derived from plants and are the same as endogenous human oestrogen.
- Dose of oestrogen varies between HRT formulations.

### Progestogens

- Protect the endometrium from oestrogenic stimulation.
- Are synthetic steroids derived from either progesterone or testosterone.
- Different HRT formulations vary in dose and type of progestogen contained:
  - Medroxyprogesterone acetate and dydrogesterone are derived from progesterone.
  - Norethisterone, levonorgestrel and norgeston are derived from testosterone.

Transdermal HRT is associated with a lower risk of VTE and stroke, and the BMS considers this form of HRT to be first line for women at increased risk of these conditions (e.g. because of medical history, age or weight).

([Post Reproductive Health, 2020;26(4):181](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053369120957514))

## HRT containing conjugated equine oestrogens

These are the ‘original HRT’. Preparations available in the UK are: Premarin and Premique low dose.

### Do they come from horses?

Yes. Conjugated equine oestrogens are extracted from the urine of pregnant mares, and contain a variety of oestrogens and oestrogen metabolites.

Research has shown that Premarin contains at least 10 oestrogens and, as they are all biologically active, conjugated preparations may be more beneficial (or stronger) than preparations containing only oestradiol ([Proceedings of the Society for Experimental Biology and Medicine, 1998;217:6](https://doi.org/10.3181/00379727-217-44199)).

### Safety concerns

A letter in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology ([British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology 2000; 49:489](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2125.2000.00172.x)) highlighted the following concerns regarding conjugated equine oestrogens:

- The exact components and their concentrations are unknown.
- The pharmacological properties and benefits are unproven.
- There may be carcinogenic potential of some known constituent metabolites, e.g. equilin metabolites.
- Conjugated equine oestrogens were the predominant preparation in the major studies linking increased breast cancer with long-term HRT use.

The authors conclude that it would seem sensible to replace oestradiol deficiency with an exact hormone rather than a cocktail of uncertain composition.

A Cochrane review evaluating the efficacy of HRT found there was no good evidence of a difference in efficacy between conventional HRT and conjugated equine oestrogens. The findings regarding adverse effects were inconsistent ([Cochrane 2016, CD 010407](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd010407.pub2)).

### Animal welfare

A number of animal welfare charities campaign against the use of conjugated equine oestrogens due to concerns about the treatment of the horses used in the drug manufacture (see [PeTA - premarin: a prescription for cruelty](https://www.peta.org/issues/animals-used-for-experimentation/animals-used-experimentation-factsheets/premarin-prescription-cruelty/)). An interesting (albeit old!) article in the Journal of Medical Ethics discusses whether it is right to prescribe equine-derived HRT when plant-based alternatives are available ([Journal of Medical Ethics, 1996;22:199](https://doi.org/10.1136/jme.22.4.199)).

### What does this mean for primary care?

We should consider conjugated equine oestrogens as second-line HRT preparations.

## ‘Bioidentical’ hormones

In 2024, the British Menopause Society updated its consensus statement on bioidentical HRT ([Bioidentical HRT - British Menopause Society](https://thebms.org.uk/publications/consensus-statements/bioidentical-hrt/)).

Bioidentical hormones are plant-derived hormones that are structurally identical to those produced by the body. This includes conventional HRT containing oestradiol or micronised progesterone (sometimes known as ‘body identical HRT’). However, the phrase ‘bioidentical’ is often used as a marketing term to describe compounded HRT preparations that are manufactured in the private sector and are not available on the NHS.

The BMS has clarified the difference between these two types of HRT:

- Compounded bioidentical HRT formulations (cBHRT).
- Regulated bioidentical hormones (rBHRT), previously known as ‘body identical HRT’.

### Compounded bioidentical hormone therapy

Manufactured by ‘specialist pharmacies’, these cBHRT preparations contain a variety of different hormones, including oestradiol, estriol and estrone, often combined with progesterone and testosterone.

Hormonal deficiency is ascertained from (unapproved) serum and salivary hormone level tests, and a ‘tailored’ replacement therapy is then delivered by lozenges, creams or vaginal preparations of varying concentration and potency.

#### Are they better than conventional HRT?

These are promoted as a ‘natural’ alternative to conventional hormone therapy, and as being better tailored to the individual woman. However, because they are marketed as ‘natural supplements’, they are not regulated by the MHRA, and lack both efficacy and safety data.

#### What are the concerns?

- The absence of evidence to support efficacy or safety.
- The unknown concentrations and potency of the constituent hormones.
- The lack of warnings on products regarding risks and side-effects.
- The potential for inadequate endometrial protection (progesterone is not always sufficiently absorbed transdermally).
- Prescribers are often not experts in the field of menopause and may not have had any recognised training.

### Regulated bioidentical hormone therapy

Regulated bioidentical hormone therapies (rBHRT) include micronised progesterone and oestradiol. These products are made in the conventional way by the pharmaceutical industry. They are authorised and regulated by the MHRA in the UK, and can be prescribed by us in primary care.

#### What are the advantages of rBHRT over HRT containing synthetic progestogens?

- rBHRT progesterones may have fewer androgenic side-effects than conventional HRT.
- Transdermal oestrogen does not increase VTE risk.
- There is observational and case–control data suggesting that micronised progesterone may reduce the risk of VTE associated with oral oestrogen when compared with conventional synthetic progestogens.
- Micronised progesterone has neutral effects on breast cell proliferation, whereas synthetic progestogens such as medroxyprogesterone acetate have a proliferative effect. Oestrogen used with micronised progesterone is associated with a lower relative risk of breast cancer than other types of combined HRT. Further data from larger studies is required to confirm these potential benefits.

#### What are the concerns?

Endometrial protection may not be as consistent with separately-administered oral progesterone. Further detail on ensuring adequate progestogen/progesterone dosing to provide endometrial protection can be found in our article *HRT regimens and progestogen doses*.

## Tibolone

Tibolone is a synthetic steroid with oestrogenic, progestogenic and androgenic properties which has been in clinical use since the early 1990s for treatment of menopausal symptoms.

Because of its androgenicity, it is sometimes used in postmenopausal women for whom low libido is an issue.

In 2016, Cochrane reviewed the short and long-term effects of tibolone in postmenopausal women. Its findings are summarised below:

### Benefits

- Tibolone is more effective than placebo but less effective than combined HRT in reducing postmenopausal symptoms.
- Tibolone is associated with a lower rate of unscheduled bleeding than combined HRT.

### Risks

(Note: most of the studies had a follow up of 2–3y, and evidence was mostly low quality.)

- Tibolone does not appear to increase the risk of breast cancer, although it does increase the risk of recurrent breast cancer in women with a history of breast cancer (see LIBERATE study: [Drug Safety Update,2009;2(7)](https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/tibolone-benefit-risk-balance)).
- Tibolone does not appear to increase CVA but may increase rates of CVA in women over 60y.
- Tibolone does not appear to increase the risk of endometrial cancer, CVD, VTE or mortality from any cause.
- Tibolone does not appear to differ significantly from combined HRT in terms of long-term risks.

### Does it actually improve libido?

Cochrane also looked at hormone therapy for treating perimenopausal sexual function ([CD009672, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009672)) and concluded that:

- Current evidence does not demonstrate an important effect of tibolone on sexual function.
- This evidence is of low quality so more research is needed.
- Conventional HRT does appear to improve sexual function in symptomatic menopausal women.

## Key Clinical Summary

> **HRT: bioidentical, conjugated and synthetic**
> Conventional HRT contains bioidentical oestrogen and a synthetic progestogen for endometrial protection.
> Regulated bioidentical HRT (rBHRT) refers to regulated and approved bioidentical oestradiol and micronised progesterone.
> Micronised progesterone may be safer and better tolerated than synthetic progestogens.
> Preparations containing equine oestrogens should be considered second-line agents.
> Compounded bioidentical HRT preparations (cBHRT) contain a mixture of different hormones. These are sold by private pharmacies, and they lack safety and efficacy data.
> Tibolone is a synthetic steroid with oestrogenic, progestogenic and androgenic properties. It may be used as an alternative to HRT, but evidence suggests that there appears to be little benefit compared with conventional HRT.

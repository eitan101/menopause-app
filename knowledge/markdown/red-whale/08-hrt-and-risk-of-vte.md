# HRT and risk of VTE

> **Layer 1 Source Reference:** [`HRT and risk of VTE 8.docx`](../../sources/red-whale/HRT%20and%20risk%20of%20VTE%208.docx)
> **Collection:** Red Whale Menopause Guidelines | **Original Format:** `DOCX` | **Review Date:** `July 2025`

*This article has been updated in July 2025.*

Whether in the form of combined hormonal contraception or as HRT, oestrogens are known to be associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE). Evidence suggests that the type of progestogen used in HRT can also modulate risk, and that oestradiol administered transdermally does not appear to have a prothrombotic effect. The risk of VTE associated with HRT is highest during the first year of use, and represents one-third of potentially fatal events attributable to HRT use ([Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol. 2010;30:340](https://doi.org/10.1161/atvbaha.109.196022)).

In this article, we examine the evidence surrounding VTE and different HRT preparations, and look at how this impacts our prescribing decisions and how we counsel our menopausal patients.

We have used British Menopause Society publications, NICE guidelines and other papers where referenced ([Post Reproductive Health 2023, 29(1):33](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691221148036); [BMS & WHC’s 2020 recommendations on hormone replacement therapy in menopausal women - British Menopause Society](https://thebms.org.uk/publications/consensus-statements/bms-whcs-2020-recommendations-on-hormone-replacement-therapy-in-menopausal-women/); [NICE, 2015 (updated 2024), NG23](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23)).

## How does HRT increase the risk of thrombosis?

### Oestrogenic effect

- The prothrombotic effect of oral oestrogen is believed to result from its first-pass metabolism through the liver following absorption in the gut.
- Hepatic metabolism of oestradiol – particularly to oestrone – is believed to impact the manufacture of coagulation factors.
- Oestrone is a potent inducer of liver protein synthesis, and high levels of oestrone are associated with increased thrombin production and increased activated protein C resistance, which potentially leads to increased blood coagulability.
- Transdermal HRT bypasses first-pass metabolism, and therefore is associated with lower oestrone and thrombin levels.
- Studies have consistently shown that oral, but NOT transdermal, oestrogen activates the coagulation cascade and inhibits anticoagulant factors in the blood.

### Progestogenic effect

- The role of progestogens in thromboembolism is unclear:
  - Low-dose progestogen formulations (such as the contraceptive pills) do not appear to be associated with increased risk of VTE.
  - Higher-dose progestogens (for example as depot or orally to control gynaecological symptoms) have shown an association with VTE.
  - The presence of progestogens in HRT also appears to impact VTE risk.
- Different progestogens have distinct pharmacological and clinical properties, and studies suggest that the type of progestogen used in combined HRT formulations may affect thrombosis risk.
- In laboratory tests, there has been no consistent evidence demonstrating any differential effect of progestogens on haemostasis.
- A prothrombotic effect may stem from progestogen-induced changes in venous wall structure, leading to increased venous stasis (as is seen in pregnancy).
- Hepatic metabolism converts norethisterone to ethinyl oestradiol, but this effect is likely to be less significant at the lower doses used in HRT.

([Maturitas 2011;70:354](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.maturitas.2011.10.002); [Arterioscler Thromb Vasc Biol, 2010;30:340](https://doi.org/10.1161/atvbaha.109.196022); [Post Reproductive Health 2023, 29(1):33](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691221148036); [Post Reproductive Health,2024;30(1):39](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691231223682))

## What does the evidence looking at HRT and VTE show?

In summary, the evidence available (detailed in the table below) suggests that:

- Oral HRT is associated with an increased risk of VTE, while transdermal HRT is not – but this is based on observational, not RCT, data.
- Oral oestradiol may be safer than conjugated equine oestrogens.
- In combined HRT, the type of progestogen has been shown to impact VTE risk.
- Of the progestogens licensed for use in HRT in the UK:
  - Micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone are likely to be the safest options.
  - Medroxyprogesterone acetate and norethisterone appear to further increase the risk of VTE in oral preparations compared with oral oestrogen alone.
  - There is limited data on the risk of levonorgestrel in HRT.
- Tibolone may not increase the risk of VTE.

## Counselling women about VTE risk and HRT

- The background risk of VTE in a menopausal woman is low: around 0.5/1000 if she is <50y, increasing to 2–3/1000 when she is >60y ([Maturitas, 2011;69:195](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.maturitas.2011.03.011)).
- Risk of VTE increases with age, and is higher if she smokes, is overweight or has an inherent genetic predisposition.
- Taking oral HRT increases the risk of VTE 2–4-fold; transdermal HRT does not appear to increase risk at all.
- Different progestogens in combined HRT may differ in their thrombotic potential, with micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appearing to be safer *(see HRT: GEMS for available formulations*).

### How should we manage women with risk factors for VTE?

The British Menopause Society and NICE recommend that:

- All women be assessed for VTE risk before being prescribed HRT.
- We consider:
  - Prescribing transdermal rather than oral HRT for women at INCREASED risk of VTE (e.g. those with a BMI >30).
  - Referring those at HIGH risk of VTE (i.e. strong family or personal history of VTE, hereditary thrombophilia) to a haematologist before prescribing HRT – *although, in primary care, we may refer to a local menopause service instead.*

([NICE, 2015 (updated 2024), NG23](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23))

## Options for high-risk women

The British Menopause Society position is that:

- There is no robust RCT evidence looking at the risk of VTE conferred by using HRT in high-risk women (those who are obese, have thrombophilia or a personal history of VTE).
- BUT epidemiological evidence suggests that transdermal HRT with micronised progesterone does not appear to increase risk above baseline.
- Clinicians should balance severity of menopausal symptoms with previous medical history, ongoing risk of VTE and use of anticoagulation.
- Treatment decisions in high-risk women may benefit from the involvement of a haematologist, and concomitant anticoagulation may be considered (*although this should be balanced by increased bleeding risk*).
- The preferred formulation in high-risk women is:
  - Low-dose transdermal oestrogen.
  - Plus oral micronised progesterone if required.
  - OR a levonorgestrel intrauterine system (*although note: there is limited data looking at VTE risk in menopausal women using an IUS as part of HRT*).

([Post Reproductive Health, 2023;29(1):33](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691221148036); [BMS & WHC’s 2020 recommendations on hormone replacement therapy in menopausal women - British Menopause Society)](https://thebms.org.uk/publications/consensus-statements/bms-whcs-2020-recommendations-on-hormone-replacement-therapy-in-menopausal-women/)

## Eligibility criteria tool for HRT?

- In an article in Post Reproductive Health in 2024, an expert group from the British Menopause Society proposed a medical eligibility tool to guide HRT prescribing.
- This would be similar to the UK medical eligibility criteria for contraception (UKMEC).
- The group chose VTE as an initial pilot area for proposed HRT guidance. It reviewed existing evidence surrounding this, and came up with a number of tables to guide prescribing in different risk factor scenarios.
- It based the categorisation scale on the UKMEC (i.e. 1 = no contraindication, 2 = benefits likely to outweigh risk, 3 = risks likely to outweigh benefit and 4 = absolute contraindication).
- It recommended that HRT MEC 1–2 could mean initiation by a non-specialist, but MEC 3 should involve specialist advice and counselling prior to prescribing.

## Is there ever a role for thrombophilia testing?

The British Menopause Society does not recommend routine thrombophilia screening prior to starting HRT, but states that testing may be justified in some circumstances, such as individuals with:

- A strong family history of VTE (multiple first-degree relatives affected by VTE under the age of 50y).
- Recurrent VTE.
- A positive family history of a specific thrombophilia.

The BMS also recommends that women >50y with a personal history of VTE within the past 12 months should also be investigated for an underlying medical cause ([Post Reproductive Health 2023, 29(1):33](https://doi.org/10.1177/20533691221148036)).

## Key Clinical Summary

> **HRT and risk of VTE**
> The risk of a VTE in a perimenopausal woman is low: around 0.5/1000.
> HRT increases the risk of VTE, but the risk appears to depend on the type and route of oestrogen, and the progestogen used.
> Observational data shows that transdermal oestrogen does not increase VTE risk; this may be due to the avoidance of first-pass liver metabolism and a neutral effect on clotting factors.
> Micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone appear to be the safest progestogens, but more data is needed regarding the relative risks of progestogens.
> NICE and the British Menopause Society recommend transdermal HRT for patients at increased risk of VTE (e.g. obese women), and that we refer high-risk women (e.g. those who have had a VTE or have thrombophilia) to a haematologist or specialist menopause clinic.

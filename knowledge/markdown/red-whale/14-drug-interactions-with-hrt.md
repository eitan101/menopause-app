# Drug interactions with HRT

> **Layer 1 Source Reference:** [`Drug interactions with HRT 14.docx`](../../sources/red-whale/Drug%20interactions%20with%20HRT%2014.docx)
> **Collection:** Red Whale Menopause Guidelines | **Original Format:** `DOCX` | **Review Date:** `August 2025`

We may think of HRT as being a low-dose replacement for physiological hormones, and therefore unlikely to have any significant issues or interactions with other drugs. However, like hormonal contraception, HRT is a pharmacologically active entity, and may therefore impact or be impacted by concurrent medications – whether due to hepatic enzyme activity or absorption issues.

In this article, we start by looking at the potential issues of GLP-1 mimetic use in women on HRT, and the British Menopause Society guidance regarding this ([British Menopause Society - NEW BMS Tool for Clinicians – Use of incretin-based therapies in women using HRT](https://thebms.org.uk/2025/04/new-bms-tool-for-clinicians-use-of-incretin-based-therapies-in-women-using-hrt/)). We also summarise a review article which examines the very little research there is looking at drug interactions with hormone replacement therapy, and the conclusions drawn ([Menopause,2023;30(8):873](https://journals.lww.com/menopausejournal/abstract/2023/08000/pharmacological_interactions_and_menopausal.13.aspx)).

*This article was updated in August 2025.*

## HRT and GLP-1 mimetics (and other incretin-based therapies)

In 2025, the BMS published a Tools for Clinicians document looking at the potential and proven issues associated with use of incretin-based therapies alongside HRT ([British Menopause Society - NEW BMS Tool for Clinicians – Use of incretin-based therapies in women using HRT](https://thebms.org.uk/2025/04/new-bms-tool-for-clinicians-use-of-incretin-based-therapies-in-women-using-hrt/)).

### What are incretin-based therapies?

Incretin-based therapies (which include glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP) agonists) mimic the actions of gut-derived hormones (incretins) that regulate appetite, satiety and insulin secretion, and are used in the treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity. Tirzepatide, semaglutide and liraglutide are also licensed in the UK for weight management. The BMS guide looks specifically at tirzepatide and semaglutide, but advises that the recommendations for those should apply to all incretin-based therapies.

For more information on incretin-based therapies, see article *Obesity: pharmacological management* on Red Whale Knowledge.

### Why is this important?

- The menopause transition is associated with an average weight increase of 2–3kg, which can lead to central weight deposition and metabolic issues.
- Over 60% of perimenopausal and menopausal women are overweight. They may seek assistance with weight loss to improve their health and wellbeing, either via the NHS or privately.
- The advent of incretin-based drugs, which are effective in reducing weight as well as treating diabetes mellitus, means that we may see increasing numbers of women on these treatments who are also taking HRT.
- A recognised side-effect of tirzepatide and semaglutide is gastrointestinal upset and delayed gastric emptying, which could reduce the absorption of oral medications:
  - Reduced absorption of oral oestrogens may result in reduced symptom control.
  - Reduced oral progestogen absorption may result in inadequate endometrial protection and increased risk of endometrial cancer. Women with obesity are already at increased risk of endometrial cancer so this is a particular concern.
  - Mismatch between oestradiol and progestogen absorption can lead to unscheduled bleeding. This should be assessed as per BMS guidelines, with a lower threshold for investigation in high-risk women (for details, see article on *Approaching and managing problematic bleeding on HRT*).
- There is no data looking at the efficacy of HRT when taken alongside incretin-based therapies, but data involving combined oral contraception shows reduced bioavailability when taken alongside tirzepatide; this is believed to result from delayed gastric emptying.

### BMS guidance on HRT in women on incretin-based therapies

The preferred HRT option for women taking incretin-based therapies is transdermal oestradiol with a non-oral progestogen for endometrial protection. For other types of HRT, see table below for advice on management.

When counselling patients, it is important to explain the limitations of the data and the rationale for the BMS guidance, which is based on extrapolation of combined oral contraception interaction data and pragmatism.

| Type of HRT | BMS recommendations |
| :--- | :--- |
| Oral oestrogen-only HRT (with or without 52mg IUS) | Transdermal oestrogen preparations are associated with a lower risk of VTE so should be used preferentially in women with a raised BMI.<br>Switch to combined transdermal preparations or transdermal oestrogen and IUS because these are unaffected by gastrointestinal upset. |
| Combined oral HRT formulations | Transdermal oestrogen preparations are associated with a lower risk of VTE so should be used preferentially in women with a raised BMI.<br>Switch to combined transdermal preparations or transdermal oestrogen and IUS because these are unaffected by gastrointestinal upset. |
| Oral oestrogen with separate oral progestogen | Transdermal oestrogen preparations are associated with a lower risk of VTE so should be used preferentially in women with a raised BMI.<br>Switch to combined transdermal preparations or transdermal oestrogen and IUS because these are unaffected by gastrointestinal upset. |
| Transdermal oestradiol with oral progestogen | Switch to a 52mg IUS or combined transdermal preparation because these provide effective endometrial protection irrespective of dose changes.<br>If a woman prefers to remain on an oral progestogen, increase dose of progestogen at initiation of incretin-based therapy for 4w, and give higher dose again for 4w after any dose increase, until dose is stable or treatment stopped. |
| Transdermal oestradiol with vaginal progesterone | No change in dose required, but note that this is an off-licence use anyway. |
| Transdermal oestrogen-only HRT | No change in dose or formulation. |
| Transdermal oestradiol with 52mg IUS | No change in dose or formulation. |
| Combined transdermal HRT | No change in dose or formulation. |

## HRT and other potential drug interactions

A systemic review in the Journal of the North American Menopause Society looked at the existing research surrounding drug interactions with HRT to guide safe therapeutic decision-making ([Menopause,2023;30(8):873](https://journals.lww.com/menopausejournal/abstract/2023/08000/pharmacological_interactions_and_menopausal.13.aspx)).

The authors used a framework based on medical eligibility criteria for contraception to designate risk of adverse interaction between HRT and a particular drug or drug class. The categories are 1–4, as detailed below:

> **Category 1**: no expected risks based on high-quality evidence, or lack of biological plausibility for risk.
> **Category 2**: acceptable use based on lack of published evidence suggesting harm, or any potential risk likely to be insignificant.
> **Category 3**: alternatives should be considered due to possible risk, but use may be justified if benefit of treatment outweighs risk.
> **Category 4**: combination should be avoided due to evidence of risk, or use is unjustifiable given impact of possible interaction.

The authors commented that:

- Data looking at interactions with HRT is scarce. Assessment of the likely interaction was largely extrapolated from studies involving hormonal contraception and from population evidence showing lack of negative interactions with concomitant use.
- HRT includes many different preparations, and these contain different constituent hormones and have differing routes of administration and dosing. They may therefore vary in their pharmacological interactions.

Some of the potentially useful findings and categories are summarised in the table below, and may be helpful when consulting with more medically-complex patients:

| Drugs | Category | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Antihypertensives | 1 for transdermal HRT<br>2 for oral HRT | Oestrogens may impact blood pressure, and dose adjustments may therefore be needed.<br>Transdermal HRT is safer than oral from a CVA risk perspective. |
| Statins | 1–2 | HRT, especially oral HRT, can increase triglycerides and cholesterol. |
| Oral antifungals | 1 for transdermal HRT<br>2 for oral HRT | No evidence of interaction with long-term co-administration, but may require lower doses of oral HRT. |
| Antiretrovirals | 2 | No studies on interaction, but may be necessary to adjust dose of HRT. |
| Corticosteroids | 2 | Oestrogens could reduce metabolism of steroids so dose reduction may be required. |
| Antipsychotics | 1 for oestrogen-only HRT<br>3 for combined HRT | Limited study data, but widely co-prescribed.<br>Concomitant progestogen may increase risk of adverse effects. |
| Immunosuppressants | 3 | No studies showing interaction, but dose adjustments may be required, according to SPCs. |
| Antiepileptic drugs | 3 for transdermal HRT<br>3/4 for oral HRT | Based on studies with hormonal contraception, enzyme-inducing drugs could reduce oestrogenic efficacy.<br>Oral HRT could increase metabolism of antiepileptic. |
| Selective oestrogen receptor modulators (e.g. tamoxifen) | 4 | Plausible biological interaction, which may have adverse side-effects. |
| Aromatase inhibitors (e.g. letrozole) | 4 | Antagonistic effect of HRT on mode of action.<br>Increased risk of recurrence if using for breast cancer. |

## Key Clinical Summary

> **Drug interactions with HRT**
> HRT may impact, or be impacted by, concurrent medication, although research in this area is limited.
> Incretin-based treatments for weight management or diabetes may theoretically impact the absorption of oral HRT.
> Reduced absorption of oral progestogen may potentially result in inadequate endometrial protection and increase the risk of endometrial cancer.
> The BMS recommends transdermal oestradiol with a non-oral progestogen in women using incretin-based treatment.
> If a woman prefers to use an oral progestogen, she should increase her dose of progestogen for 4 weeks after starting an incretin-based drug, and with any increase in dose.
> There is limited data looking at other drug interactions with HRT.

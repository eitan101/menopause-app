# HRT and osteoporosis

> **Layer 1 Source Reference:** [`HRT and osteoporosis 10.docx`](../../sources/red-whale/HRT%20and%20osteoporosis%2010.docx)
> **Collection:** Red Whale Menopause Guidelines | **Original Format:** `DOCX` | **Review Date:** `August 2026`

One of the downsides to menopause is its deleterious effect on bones, and one of the benefits of HRT is its positive role in preventing this. But what is the therapeutic place of HRT in the management of osteoporosis, and are the benefits outweighed by the risks?

This article provides a short overview of osteoporosis in the context of menopause, and looks specifically at the role of HRT.

We have included information on risk assessment and diagnosis drawn from 2026 NICE guidance ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). We have also included advice from the [National Osteoporosis Guideline Group](https://www.nogg.org.uk/), [SIGN 2015 (updated 2021), 142](https://www.sign.ac.uk/guidelines/management-of-osteoporosis-and-the-prevention-of-fragility-fractures/) and the [British Menopause Society](https://thebms.org.uk/) where relevant.

There are more detailed articles on the investigation and management of osteoporosis on Red Whale Knowledge.

*This article was updated in August 2026.*

## Osteoporosis: definition

Osteoporosis is characterised by low bone mass and structural deterioration of bone tissue, resulting in increased bone fragility and susceptibility to fragility fractures.

The diagnosis of osteoporosis is a based on a bone mineral density T-score of -2.5 at the lumbar spine, total hip or femoral neck ([SIGN 142, 2015](https://www.sign.ac.uk/media/1812/sign-142-osteoporosis-v3.pdf) (revised 2021)). A T-score of -1 to -2.5 gives a diagnosis of osteopenia. Osteoporosis prevalence is estimated at 10% of the world population, but 30% of postmenopausal women are affected ([Medicina 2024;60:2109](https://doi.org/10.3390/medicina60122109)).

T-scores are based on the mean for a healthy young woman. A T-score of -2.5 is 2.5 standard deviations below the mean for a healthy young woman. However, a T-score can only ever be a surrogate marker for fracture risk, and relying on bone mineral density alone misses the important clinical reality that most people who have a fragility fracture do not have an osteoporotic T-score ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)). In managing osteoporosis, we need to look beyond T-scores, and prioritise fracture prevention and treatment.

## Fragility fractures

- Fragility factures occur following low-level trauma, and commonly affect the spine, hip, shoulder and wrist (although can affect other bones) ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)).
- The consequence of fragility fractures can be substantial; they can cause pain, disability, reduced quality of life and shortened life expectancy ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)).
- Hip fractures nearly always require hospitalisation. 20% of people die within 12m of the injury, and 50% suffer permanent disability ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)).
- Due to our ageing population, osteoporotic fractures have a huge impact on the healthcare economy. In 2019, osteoporosis costs accounted for 2.4% of healthcare spending in the UK, at around £5.4bn ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)).

## When to assess fracture risk

NICE makes recommendations about who needs fracture risk assessment based on the age of the patient, their menopausal status and whether they have additional clinical risk factors. NICE recommends that we assess fracture risk for some (presumably those considered to be at higher risk), and *consider* risk assessment for other groups ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). *(We have outlined its recommendations for women below; for details of NICE recommendations for everyone else, see main osteoporosis article).*

**NICE fracture risk assessment recommendations ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259))**

| Recommendation | Target Population |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Assess fracture risk in:** | • People ≥50y **and** postmenopausal woman <50y who have any of:<br>◦ Previous fragility fracture.<br>◦ Current or frequent oral/systemic steroid use (excluding use for adrenal insufficiency in Addison’s disease): ≥5mg prednisolone daily for >3m or equivalent, or intermittent use of higher doses (NICE gives the example of ≥3 rescue packs for a respiratory condition).<br>• People <50y who are not postmenopausal if they have had either:<br>◦ A previous hip or vertebral fracture.<br>◦ ≥2 major osteoporotic fractures. |
| ***Consider*** **assessing fracture risk in:** | • Women ≥65y and men aged ≥75y, even if no additional risk factors.<br>• People aged 50–74y who have any additional risk factors.<br>• Postmenopausal women <65y who have any additional risk factors (outlined in table below).<br>• People <50y who are not postmenopausal and who have:<br>◦ Untreated early menopause/premature ovarian insufficiency OR<br>◦ Previous non-hip, non-vertebral fragility fracture OR<br>◦ Current or frequent oral/systemic steroid use (≥5mg prednisolone for >3m or equivalent, or intermittent use of higher doses (unless this is for adrenal insufficiency in Addison’s disease)). |

### Risk factors for fragility fracture/osteoporosis

NICE outlines risk factors for fragility fracture that should prompt risk assessment in the groups outlined above ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). There are LOTS of factors to consider, and we have highlighted some additional risks identified within the NOGG guidance which are not covered by NICE ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)).

**Risk factors for fragility fracture/osteoporosis ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259))**

- ≥2 falls in past 12m.
- Hip fracture in a first-degree relative (especially if the relative was <80y at the time of injury).
- Previous fragility fracture.
- Low BMI (<18.5).
- Current smoker.
- Alcohol intake >14u/week.
- Current or frequent oral/systemic steroid use (excluding use for adrenal insufficiency in Addison’s disease): ≥5mg daily prednisolone for >3m or equivalent, or intermittent use of higher doses (NICE gives the example of ≥3 rescue packs for a respiratory condition). *NOGG wants us to risk assess those using steroids at any dose for ≥3m.*

**Wider risk factors (including secondary causes of osteoporosis)**

| System / Category | Risk Factors |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Endocrine** | • Hypogonadism (including untreated early menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency, or treatment with aromatase inhibitors or androgen deprivation therapy).<br>• Hyperthyroidism.<br>• Hyperparathyroidism.<br>• Hyperprolactinaemia.<br>• Cushing's disease.<br>• Diabetes (type 1 and 2). |
| **GI** | • Coeliac disease.<br>• Crohn's disease and other inflammatory bowel diseases.<br>• Chronic liver disease.<br>• Chronic pancreatitis.<br>• Other causes of malabsorption *(NOGG gives the example of bariatric surgery, and also suggests screening for fracture risk in those with confirmed calcium or vitamin D deficiency).* |
| **Hematological** | • Myeloma.<br>• Haemoglobinopathies.<br>• Systemic mastocystosis. |
| **Neurological** | • Parkinson’s disease (and parkinsonism).<br>• Neurological conditions causing prolonged immobility (e.g. MS, stroke).<br>• *NOGG adds depression, epilepsy and learning disability.* |
| **Respiratory** | • Cystic fibrosis.<br>• COPD. |
| **Rheumatology and musculoskeletal** | • Rheumatoid arthritis.<br>• Other inflammatory arthropathies, e.g. SLE or spondyloarthritis.<br>• *NOGG adds:*<br>◦ *Height loss ≥4cm.*<br>◦ *Thoracic kyphosis.*<br>◦ *Muscle disease (muscular dystrophy, sarcopenia) or lower limb amputation.* |
| **Other** | • Inherited metabolic diseases associated with osteoporosis (e.g. homocystinuria).<br>• Eating disorders related to low BMI.<br>• CKD 4 and 5.<br>• Prolonged immobility.<br>• Other medications associated with increased fracture risk (e.g. anticonvulsants, SSRIs, glitazones, PPIs, antiretroviral medicines). |

## How to assess fracture risk

A number of risk assessment tools have been developed to identify individuals at risk of fragility fracture. These are derived from epidemiological data and validated in specific populations so may be limited in accuracy.

The two main tools used in the UK are:

- [FRAX (Fracture Risk Assessment Tool)](https://fraxplus.org/calculation-tool).
- [QFracture](https://qfracture.org/).

NICE says that we can use either tool for people aged 40–90y, but should use QFracture in people aged 30–39y because FRAX is not validated in this age group ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). Below 30y, we should seek specialist advice. Whichever tool we choose, we should stick with it throughout that person's care.

FRAX is integrated into most clinical systems in the UK, and maps directly to NOGG treatment threshold graphs.

*It is worth noting that FRAX includes a slightly different list of risk factors in its tool when compared with NICE and NOGG (in the boxes above). It has a higher threshold for alcohol intake, at ≥3u/day (21u/week). We should still risk assess people who meet the NICE criteria outlined above, but only tick the box within FRAX if they meet the FRAX criteria.*

NOGG recommends adjusting FRAX to take into account additional risk factors not included in the tool ([NOGG, 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)). These include common conditions such as type 2 diabetes, Parkinson’s disease and recurrent falls.

We discuss the pros and cons of each tool, and how to adjust FRAX for risk factors, in more detail in the main *Osteoporosis* article.

## Who needs DXA scan?

### Straight to DXA scan

NICE recommends that we proceed straight to DXA scan, with or without using a risk assessment tool, for those ≥30y with ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)):

- Hip or vertebral fragility fracture.
- Recurrent fragility fracture.
- Major osteoporotic fracture within the past 2 years.

NICE guidance on breast cancer recommends a DXA scan in all women starting aromatase inhibitors who are not already on bisphosphonates ([NICE 2018, updated 2025 NG101)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng101). However, the NICE osteoporosis guideline treats aromatase inhibitors the same as any other risk factor, suggesting that we consider initial risk assessment in postmenopausal women and women aged ≥50y *(*see *drug dilemma* below for more detail)*.*

### After fracture risk assessment

In people who have had a fracture risk assessment calculated in FRAX or QFracture, NICE recommends that we:

- Consider a DXA scan in people with a 10-year risk of MAJOR osteoporotic fracture of ≥10%.
- Do a DXA scan if starting treatment for osteoporosis, as a baseline for monitoring treatment (in most cases, do not delay starting treatment to get the scan – see *Osteoporosis* article for more detail).
  - If getting a DXA scan, some groups will be eligible for a DXA-based vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) at the same time.

*Screening for vertebral fractures is an important change in the guidance and it makes good sense. Vertebral fractures are the most common type of fragility fracture, but many go undetected – and therefore untreated. We may need to provide our secondary care colleagues with information about, for example, height loss, kyphosis or history of back/rib pain to prompt this assessment.*

> **Who should have a VFA scan at the same time as being referred for a DXA? ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259))**
>
> - Women ≥60y and men ≥70y.
> - Younger people (men <70y and women <60y) with any of:
>   - Previous major osteoporotic fracture.
>   - Current or frequent use of systemic glucocorticoids (e.g. ≥5mg prednisolone per day or equivalent for >3m, or intermittent use of higher doses, unless used for replacement in adrenal insufficiency).
>   - Back or rib pain with no identified cause.
>   - Change in body shape: kyphosis, height loss >4cm, rounded shoulders.
>   - If the DXA is suspicious for vertebral fracture.
>   - Very low BMD for their age (Z-score <-2).

NOGG recommends that we map the FRAX score to age-dependent graphs to determine the need for DXA scan and further treatment ([NOGG, 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)). We have included details on how to do this in our main *Osteoporosis* article. NICE chose not to do this because it felt that a single threshold was simpler for non-specialist clinicians to implement. NICE comments that the single 10% threshold will result in fewer DXA scans being undertaken and an economic saving when compared with NOGG.

## Investigation of osteoporosis

[NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3) suggests the following baseline investigations after diagnosis of osteoporosis and fragility fractures in postmenopausal women and in men aged ≥50y:

- FBC, creatinine, LFTs (transaminases), bone profile, TFTs, inflammatory markers (ESR or CRP), vitamin D, phosphate.
- Spine X-ray or lateral DXA to rule out asymptomatic vertebral fractures.

NICE does not outline recommended blood tests to investigate osteoporosis in its diagnosis and investigation guidance ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). It does make recommendations on vertebral fracture assessment, as outlined above.

We should refer to secondary care for investigation if we diagnose osteoporosis before the menopause. Wider testing may be recommended in some groups. See *Osteoporosis* article for details.

## Maintaining bone health: lifestyle advice

NOGG 2024 guidance and the earlier 2021 SIGN guidance are closely aligned here ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3), [SIGN 142, 2021](https://www.sign.ac.uk/media/1812/sign-142-osteoporosis-v3.pdf)). We should recommend that people:

- Stop smoking.
- Reduce excessive alcohol consumption (NOGG recommends ≤2u/day).
- Do regular weight-bearing exercise (in addition, balance training, flexibility and stretching exercises may reduce falls risk).
- Maintain healthy weight and eat a balanced diet.
- Ensure adequate consumption of calcium and exposure to vitamin D.
  - NOGG recommends 700mg/day calcium, ideally through diet. Universal supplementation is not needed.
  - Correct any vitamin D deficiency. Consider supplementation in ‘at-risk’ groups (see *Vitamin D: GEMS*).
- NOGG also advises that a falls risk assessment is done in anyone with osteoporosis.

## Management of osteoporosis

There are a number of different therapeutic options for osteoporosis, and these are addressed by NICE in separate technology appraisals ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)). Each works in different ways, and they have their own risks, benefits and limitations. Options include ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3)):

- Oral bisphosphonates – these are generally first line (e.g. alendronic acid, risedronate, ibandronic acid).
- IV bisphosphonates (zoledronate).
- HRT (see below).
- Denosumab.
- Teriparatide.
- Raloxifene.
- Strontium.

See that *Osteoporosis* article again for details!

## The role of HRT in osteoporosis

Some HRT preparations are licensed for osteoporosis prophylaxis, but none are licensed for treatment of osteoporosis ([NG23, 2015](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23) (updated 2026)).

### HRT: mode of action and efficacy

HRT reduces the rate of bone turnover and therefore has an antiresorptive effect on bone. There is good evidence that HRT has a significant protective effect against osteoporosis and related fragility fractures – including hip and vertebral.

### Supporting evidence

- The [NICE 2019 evidence review on the long-term benefits of HRT](https://www.nice.org.uk/consultations/672/10/long-term-benefits-and-risks-of-hormone-replacement-therapy) looked at cohort studies, systematic reviews and a Cochrane review, and found that 9 out of 10 showed lower fracture risk or increased bone mineral density with HRT use.
- A 2025 Cochrane review comments that evidence on HRT and fracture risk is uncertain because many of the larger trials (such as WHI) excluded women with severe osteoporosis and did not routinely collect bone mineral density data ([Cochrane 2025 CD004143](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004143.pub6/full#CD004143-sec-0156)). However, it was able to show from WHI data that, when compared with placebo:
  - Continuous combined HRT probably reduced overall fracture risk from around 111 women to 87 women (24 fewer fractures) in every 1000 over 5 years.
  - Oestrogen-only HRT probably reduced overall fracture risk from about 141 down to 103 women (38 fewer fractures) in every 1000 women over 7 years.
- A meta-analysis of 28 RCTs found a significant reduction in total fractures with HRT: RR 0.74 (CI 0.69–0.80) ([Menopause 2016;23(4):461](https://doi.org/10.1097/gme.0000000000000519)).
- A 2002 meta-analysis of osteoporosis therapies that looked at pooled results found a non-significant trend towards a reduction in fracture risk ([Endocr Rev 2002;23:529](https://doi.org/10.1210/er.2001-5002)):
  - For vertebral fracture: by 34%.
  - For non-vertebral fracture: by 13%.
  - The authors comment that statistical significance may be impacted by precision of reduction estimates due to the sample sizes involved.
- The bone-protective effect of oestrogen is dose and duration related, and appears to decline after discontinuation of treatment. There are, however, some studies which indicate that the benefit may persist longer-term ([Post Reproductive Health 2020;26(4):181](https://doi.org/10.1177/2053369120957514)).

### So, when do we prescribe HRT for osteoporosis?

This depends on which guideline you look at:

| Guideline | Indication for HRT |
| :--- | :--- |
| [NICE (NG23, 2015](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng23)) | • Management of premature ovarian insufficiency (menopause occurring <40y).<br>• To treat menopausal symptoms, and we should inform women of the reduced risk of fragility fractures as a secondary benefit. |
| [SIGN (142, 2021](https://www.sign.ac.uk/media/1812/sign-142-osteoporosis-v3.pdf)) | • HRT and tibolone may be considered for the prevention of fractures in younger postmenopausal women, but only after an individual assessment of the risks vs. benefits and (*presumably, because SIGN doesn’t stipulate*) if the threshold for bisphosphonates is not met.<br>• Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time. |
| [NOGG (2024)](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v3) | • HRT is a first-line treatment option in postmenopausal women ≤60y who are low risk for malignant and thromboembolic disease.<br>• Consider stopping over age 60y after individual assessment of risks vs. benefits.<br>• When HRT is stopped, reassess fracture risk and offer alternative osteoporosis treatment if appropriate. |
| [British Menopause Society Consensus Statement, 2023](https://thebms.org.uk/publications/consensus-statements/premature-ovarian-insufficiency/)) | • Consider DXA scan at diagnosis of premature ovarian insufficiency.<br>• HRT is the first-line therapeutic intervention for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis in women with premature ovarian insufficiency (<40y) and early menopause (aged 40–45y).<br>• Consider HRT for osteoporosis prevention in menopausal and postmenopausal women (including those >60y) who benefit from HRT for menopausal symptom management. |

### In summary

We can offer HRT as an option to prevent or treat bone loss associated with menopause if the benefits outweigh the risks for an individual woman.

## Drug dilemma: what about medroxyprogesterone acetate (depot contraceptive injection)?

Depot medroxyprogesterone used long term (>2y) *can* reduce bone density, but this effect reverses when treatment is stopped. Even for older women (SIGN osteoporosis guidance defines this as over 44y!), studies show only a small increase in absolute risk of fracture. Women on depot contraceptives do not need fracture risk assessment in the absence of other risk factors.

The CoRSH supports MHRA advice that, for contraception ([CoRSH - progestogen-only injectables guidance](https://www.cosrh.org/Public/Public/Documents/fsrh-ceu-guidance-progestogen-only-injectables.aspx)):

- In women <18y, depot contraceptive injection may be used first line if other options are unsuitable or unacceptable.
- A re-evaluation of the risks and benefits should be carried out every 2 years.
- For women with significant medical risk factors for osteoporosis, other methods of contraception should be considered.

CoRSH guidance states that the use of depot contraceptives in women >45y is UKMEC2 (the advantages generally outweigh the risks) [(UKMEC 2025](https://www.cosrh.org/Public/Public/Standards-and-Guidance/uk-medical-eligibility-criteria-for-contraceptive-use-ukmec.aspx)). It is generally advised that women switch to another method at age 50y, but, if a woman wishes to continue, this may be appropriate providing she has been advised of the potential risks.

## Drug dilemma: aromatase inhibitors

Aromatase inhibitor use is associated with increased rates of bone loss, at rates of 1–3% per year ([NOGG 2024](https://www.nogg.org.uk/full-guideline)).

When starting aromatase inhibitors for breast cancer, there are some inconsistencies between guidelines.

In its breast cancer guidance, NICE says ([NICE 2018, updated 2025 NG101)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng101):

- Offer a baseline DXA scan to any woman starting treatment with an aromatase inhibitor who is not already on bisphosphonates.

The NICE osteoporosis guideline treats aromatase inhibitors the same as any other risk factor ([NICE 2026 NG259](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng259)):

- Consider initial risk assessment for postmenopausal women and women 50–64y on aromatase inhibitors, and arrange DXA according to usual thresholds. (*Women ≥65y should already have been considered for risk assessment on the basis of age alone.)*
- Consider fast-tracking DXA within 6 weeks if threshold for requesting a bone density scan is met.
- If a DXA scan has been done, consider starting drug treatment for bone protection at a T-score of ≤–1.5 due to the known risk of accelerated bone loss. This means treating some people with osteopenia.

NOGG says we should assess baseline fracture risk, ticking the box for ‘secondary causes’ of osteoporosis on FRAX, and including bone mineral density assessment ‘where practical’.

- Those at high risk (in the red zone on NOGG graph) should be started on bone protection treatment with bisphosphonates or denosumab.
- Those with a FRAX probability near to (but below) the intervention threshold on NOGG graphs, or people who will also be on other drugs that might increase risk of osteoporosis (such as steroids), should be considered for a FRAX and DXA scan after 12–24 months on treatment to reassess risk.

## Key Clinical Summary

> **HRT and osteoporosis**
> - Postmenopausal women are at increased risk of osteoporosis due to the combination of age and loss of oestrogen production.
> - We should not routinely assess fracture risk in menopausal women <65y unless they have risk factors.
> - We should encourage menopausal women to optimise their bone strength by adopting healthy lifestyle measures.
> - We can offer HRT as an option to prevent or treat bone loss associated with menopause if the benefits outweigh the risks for an individual woman.
> - If a woman is starting aromatase inhibitors, be aware of risk of accelerated bone loss, and take steps to assess bone health and minimise harms.
